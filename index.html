Price is set by the square foot. Cost runs on hours and miles.

At some sites the company pays its vendor more than the customer pays the company. My read is that this is a network problem. Each site's price and its cost are driven by different things, and nobody measures the gap site by site. I'd measure it, put a dollar figure on the cause behind each loss, and change the operating model where those causes cluster.

Prepared by Ayush ParikhOctober 2026Format: written brief with a working prototype

Every number on this page comes from a synthetic 2,000-site portfolio I generated to test the method, so the method is the point and the figures are illustrative. Wage inputs start from the BLS May 2024 median for janitors ($17.27/hr). Workloads assume production rates of 2,400 to 3,800 sq ft per hour depending on building type. In practice I'd replace these with the company's own workloading standards.

Q1Framing the problem

Two meters that only share two inputs

A site's price is fixed at signing from square footage, visits per week and a national rate card. Its cost moves every week with verified hours on site, the local wage, drive time from the vendor's last stop, how many vendors compete in the area, extra work nobody billed, and re-cleans after failed inspections.

Only two of those inputs appear on both sides. Everything else is risk the company carries without pricing it. Contracts without an annual escalator make it worse every year as wages rise.

So I'd frame the work as three questions, in order:

  1. Which sites lose money once every cost is counted, and by how much?
  2. Why, in dollars per driver, for each one?
  3. Which of those drivers share a structural cause that a different operating model would remove?

Q4Root causes · prototype

Split every dollar of loss, then look for the pattern

For each site I'd build a monthly P&L and break the vendor bill into six parts: base labor at the fair local rate, hours paid but not verified by geofence, the vendor's rate premium over the local benchmark, windshield time, unbilled scope, and rework. On the revenue side, actual price is compared with a should-price from the same workload model. The biggest gap gives the site its archetype. The decomposition is my hypothesis, so I wouldn't let it grade itself. On real data I'd also train a gradient-boosted model on site margin, with SHAP values per site, and check that both methods agree on the top driver. Where they disagree, an analyst reviews the site.

I'd also run the cut by vendor, by sales rep and by contract year. If one rep's deals or one vintage of contracts dominates the loss list, the cause is upstream of operations and the fix is in pricing.

Q5Proposed solution

Run it as a network of zones, not 2,000 contracts

Each archetype gets its own play. The counts and dollars below come from the prototype.

Six changes to the operating model

  1. Zone contractsCluster sites into zones by drive time (H3 cells plus a routing check). Award each zone to one anchor vendor with guaranteed volume and an optimized route. Vendors trade rate for density, and island sites ride along with good ones.
  2. Pay for verified workPay on geofence-verified hours plus passed inspections, with a quality bonus pool per zone. Paid-but-unworked hours stop without anyone policing each visit.
  3. Should-cost pricingPrice every quote and renewal from workload, local wage and a density index. Add an isolation surcharge and a CPI escalator. Sales sees the margin floor before signing.
  4. Right-sized frequencyMove low-traffic sites from fixed 5x a week to condition-based tiers driven by inspection and occupancy signals. Customers pay for an outcome, not a visit count.
  5. Density-aware growthPoint sales at prospects near island sites. Every new contract nearby turns a loss into a stop on a route.
  6. An exit ruleA site still negative after two levers and one renewal attempt gets restructured or exited on notice.

Q6Technology, AI and automation

One margin engine feeding five everyday tools

  • Photo AI on proof of workA vision model scores cleanliness from the photos Peazy already collects and flags reused or off-site photos.
  • Geofence anomaly detectionFlags short visits, missing visits and paid hours that don't match time on site, every night.
  • LLM document readingPulls scope, escalators and renewal dates out of contract PDFs, and line items out of vendor invoices.
  • LLM issue triageSorts free-text service issues into root causes (missed area, supply, access, staffing) so rework has a reason attached.
  • Zone builder and routingH3 clustering plus vehicle routing (OR-Tools) proposes zones and the route that serves them.
  • Renewal assistantDrafts a repricing proposal with the site's own evidence for an account manager to approve. A person always signs off.

Q8What I'd build first

The site P&L table, then one pilot zone

Everything above depends on one table: every site, every month, price against fully loaded cost. It uses data the company already has, so it's the cheapest place to start, and it decides where the pilot goes.

Model notes: 2,000 synthetic sites, about 80% spread across 50 metros and 20% in small towns. Fair vendor rate = local wage × 1.30 burden × 1.22 vendor margin.
